Debt litigation · Understanding the evidence

A stack of records without an explanation

A packet of numbers, perhaps one relevant line, and no one willing to show the defendant where that line was.

John Rigler · Recollection recorded 22 October 2026 · AI-assisted research and drafting

The printed spreadsheet

In a separate debt case, John Rigler recalls appearing before a judge in a court at a higher level than the one in his mediation account. He estimates receiving twenty to forty pages that appeared to be a printed spreadsheet. He describes the appearance of an Excel worksheet wider and longer than a page, printed to PDF across many pages, with numbers separated from the headings needed to read them. No native spreadsheet or received packet has been supplied to verify the format or printing method.

Where is the line about me?

Rigler thinks only about one line in the packet concerned him. He understood the inclusion of the whole record as an attempt to provide completeness. But, he recalls, opposing counsel would not even navigate the pages to identify and show that line. His request was therefore more basic than asking for an explanation of accounting methods: which entry related to him, where was it, and how did it support the demand?

He recalls receiving no satisfactory explanation and no expectation from the court that counsel would provide the explanation he sought. He understood the court to have accepted the records as evidence despite that missing explanation. Rigler had assumed that someone presenting the packet would at least be able to locate the portion being used against him.

The judge's direction to subpoena the creator

Rigler clarifies that the judge told him he should have subpoenaed the person who created the records, and that the creator was the only person who could explain them. This is his recollection of the judge's instruction, not merely his own later suggestion. No transcript has been supplied to establish the exact wording or scope of that instruction.

He questions what would happen if that person had died or could not be reached: would the records lose their force, or could they remain accepted while their explanation was inaccessible? What the records actually prove examines the distinction between a business-record foundation and the knowledge needed to explain particular entries or calculations.

The firm identification remains tentative

Rigler recalls the law firm's name as “Rausch and Strom.” A firm named Rausch Sturm LLP identifies itself as representing creditors in debt collection.[1] This is a possible spelling match, not confirmation of which firm appeared in this case. The petition, correspondence, or signature block would establish that identity. The recollection also does not establish whether the law firm itself was a named plaintiff or represented a separate claimant.

Receiving the pages did not answer the question

The practical problem in this account begins with relevance and intelligibility. A complete packet still needs a usable path to the entry being relied on. If a column loses its heading at a page break, a reader can see a number without knowing whether it is a charge, payment, interest entry, adjustment, or running balance. Producing more pages does not supply those meanings. Even an accurately preserved export can become difficult to use when its printed layout breaks the relationship between labels and values.

Rigler's expectation was understandable as an expectation about communication: someone relying on a set of figures should be able to show the path through them. The procedural role of the lawyer, the foundation for admitting a record, and the explanation needed to test a calculation are separate questions. The case record would show which of those questions the court was actually deciding.

The work of reconstructing someone else's demand

A poorly presented account can leave the recipient reconstructing the creditor's explanation before being able to challenge it. The person must work out which pages connect, restore headings, identify codes, distinguish totals from transactions, and locate payments or credits. That work can consume the time otherwise available to examine whether the demanded amount follows from the records.

Rigler describes this as one-sided: the claimant could rely on the packet, while he was told to obtain its creator's testimony even though counsel had not shown him the relevant line. The Commons' criticism concerns that allocation of work. Before a defendant can test an entry, someone must identify the entry and the claim it is supposed to support.

This is another form of administrative burden. The concern can be investigated through the packet's layout and the answers actually supplied. No conclusion about deliberate concealment follows merely from the number of pages or from a formatting failure.

The connection to unsupported rent acceleration is the need for a traceable calculation. The connection to credit reporting as collection pressure is the difference between repeating an amount and explaining its basis. These are shared questions across separate cases.

Business records · Explanation and proof

What the records actually prove

Receiving a packet, establishing its evidentiary foundation, understanding its entries, and proving a claimed balance each call for different work.

John Rigler · 22 October 2026 · AI-assisted research and drafting

Locate the entry and connect it to the claim

Rigler's account of an unexplained spreadsheet packet includes his estimate that about one line concerned him, counsel's unwillingness to locate that line, and the judge's instruction that he should have subpoenaed the creator for an explanation. His objection begins with a concrete question: which page and row concern him, and how do they establish the amount claimed?

Asking a party to identify the entry it relies on is different from asking its lawyer to testify about how the record was created. An affidavit supporting a record's foundation does not itself give the reader a page reference, restore a missing heading, or explain the connection between a figure and the demand. Including an entire worksheet may preserve context while leaving those questions unanswered.

Texas Rules 401 and 402 address relevance and the exclusion of irrelevant evidence. Rule 611(a) calls for reasonable judicial control of presentation to aid truth-finding and avoid wasted time.[2] These provisions frame questions about an understandable presentation; they do not establish a violation in this unidentified case.

The Commons' concern is the work shifted to the recipient: reconstructing the claimant's route through the packet before being able to challenge it. The foundation for admitting records, their connection to a particular person, and whether they prove the requested amount each need examination.

The lawyer's role and the records' foundation

Texas Rules 803(6) and 902(10) permit a qualifying business-record foundation through a custodian, another qualified witness, or a compliant affidavit or declaration. Recordkeeping, knowledge, timing, trustworthiness, and service requirements apply.[2]

That structure can explain a proceeding in which litigation counsel offers records without being their creator or keeper. It does not establish what happened in Rigler's case. An affidavit, a live witness's testimony, counsel's argument, and the court's ruling would need to be identified separately.

The original creator is not always required

In Star Electricity, Inc. v. Northpark Office Tower, LP, the First Court of Appeals explained that a witness establishing the business-record foundation need not have created the document or worked for its creator. Knowledge of how the records were prepared could suffice. The court held that the invoices there were authenticated by an affidavit and should not have been excluded on the challenged ground.[3]

This is a limited comparison from another case. It explains why identifying a competent records witness can involve someone other than the person who originally entered data or printed an export. It does not establish that any particular affidavit or witness in Rigler's case was sufficient. The relevant practical question is who can competently explain the records and calculation, and what procedure makes that explanation available.

If the creator has died or cannot be reached

Rule 803 applies irrespective of the declarant's availability. A creator's death or unavailability does not by itself disqualify business records; the exception's conditions still apply.[2]

A person familiar with a recordkeeping system may establish its foundation while a different person has the knowledge needed to explain a particular calculation. A categorical rule that only the creator can supply any explanation does not follow from the authorities reviewed. The case record would need to show whether the judge was identifying a particular knowledgeable witness or asserting such a general restriction.

The practical asymmetry deserves examination: a party may use a permitted foundation to offer records, while a recipient seeking further answers must identify a knowledgeable person and understand how to obtain that person's testimony. Unavailability can make that task harder without automatically excluding the records. The Commons' question is what meaningful route remained for testing the figures, and what cost that route imposed.

What kind of spreadsheet is it?

Under Rule 1001(d), accurate electronic output can constitute an original. Rule 1006 permits voluminous-record summaries with access to originals or duplicates. Rule 901 addresses a record's claimed identity.[2]

A routine account export and a summary prepared for litigation may therefore raise different questions. The label “spreadsheet” does not identify which kind Rigler received. Nor does admission, standing alone, tell a reader which entries establish the requested amount. The foundation for receiving a record and the conclusions drawn from its contents need their own explanations.

Questions for an understandable account

The Commons proposes the following reading questions. They make the calculation inspectable; the case's claims and governing procedure determine which evidence is legally necessary.

QuestionWhat the explanation should connect
Where is the entry relied on?The original page and row, the identifier connecting that entry to the defendant, and the reason it matters to the claim.
Whose account and which period?Account identifier, relevant person, beginning and ending dates, and the claimant's asserted connection to the account.
What do the columns and codes mean?Each heading, unit, transaction code, sign convention, and continuation across page breaks.
How does the balance change?The starting balance, charges, interest and fees, payments, credits, adjustments, and ending balance, without counting subtotals twice.
How does it support the demand?The specific rows and other records relied on for each part of the requested amount, including any difference between the export's ending date and the demand date.

A legible export with repeated headings, a code legend, and a reconciliation to the demand would address much of the communication problem described in the recollection. If a person still cannot follow the calculation, the missing link should be identified precisely. A general complaint about a large packet leaves less to examine than a specific missing heading, unexplained code, omitted credit, or unreconciled total.

Preserve the original and label the explanation

For a Commons case record, retain the exact packet received and its delivery context. A clearer transcription, reflowed table, or reconciliation can be a separately identified derivative with references back to original pages. It should not silently replace the received evidence. The original layout matters when the complaint concerns that layout.

A CID can identify the exact bytes of an unreadable export. Byte verification does not restore missing headings, identify a competent witness, or reconcile a balance. The explanatory article must do that additional work and show the reader how its interpretation connects to the preserved source.

This connects to the burden before trial: a person may need procedural knowledge to obtain an answer to a factual question. It also connects to the basis and calculation behind a charge and the cumulative cost of challenging it.
