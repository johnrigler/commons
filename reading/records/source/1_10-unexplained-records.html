A stack of records without an explanation

What can a defendant examine when a large packet of numbers arrives without a usable account of what the numbers mean?

John Rigler · Recollection recorded 22 October 2026 · AI-assisted research and drafting

The printed spreadsheet

In a separate debt case, John Rigler recalls appearing before a judge in a court at a higher level than the one in his mediation account. He estimates receiving twenty to forty pages that appeared to be a printed spreadsheet. He remembers numbers continuing across pages without their headings lining up correctly, leaving parts of the packet difficult to interpret.

He wanted the opposing lawyer to go through the pages and explain them. He recalls that the lawyer could not supply a satisfactory explanation, and that the court did not expect the lawyer to explain the document in the way he had anticipated. Rigler had assumed that a lawyer presenting records accepted by the court would be able to explain their contents.

Looking back, he thinks calling the person who created the document might have helped. That is his retrospective assessment. The companion article, What the records actually prove, examines why a qualified records witness need not be the original creator and why presenting a document does not itself make the lawyer its records witness.

The firm identification remains tentative

Rigler recalls the law firm's name as “Rausch and Strom.” A firm named Rausch Sturm LLP identifies itself as representing creditors in debt collection.[1] This is a possible spelling match, not confirmation of which firm appeared in this case. The petition, correspondence, or signature block would establish that identity. The recollection also does not establish whether the law firm itself was a named plaintiff or represented a separate claimant.

Receiving the pages did not answer the question

The practical problem in this account is intelligibility. If a column loses its heading at a page break, a reader can see a number without knowing whether it is a charge, payment, interest entry, adjustment, or running balance. Producing more pages does not supply those meanings. Even an accurately preserved export can become difficult to use when its printed layout breaks the relationship between labels and values.

Rigler's expectation was understandable as an expectation about communication: someone relying on a set of figures should be able to show the path through them. The procedural role of the lawyer, the foundation for admitting a record, and the explanation needed to test a calculation are separate questions. The case record would show which of those questions the court was actually deciding.

The work of reconstructing someone else's demand

A poorly presented account can leave the recipient reconstructing the creditor's explanation before being able to challenge it. The person must work out which pages connect, restore headings, identify codes, distinguish totals from transactions, and locate payments or credits. That work can consume the time otherwise available to examine whether the demanded amount follows from the records.

This is another form of administrative burden. The concern can be investigated through the packet's layout and the answers actually supplied. No conclusion about deliberate concealment follows merely from the number of pages or from a formatting failure.

The connection to unsupported rent acceleration is the need for a traceable calculation. The connection to credit reporting as collection pressure is the difference between repeating an amount and explaining its basis. These are shared questions across separate cases.
