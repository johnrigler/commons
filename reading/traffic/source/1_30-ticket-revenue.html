Municipal finance · Highway enforcement · Institutional incentives

When citations fund the town

A government that depends on enforcement receipts has a financial interest in the activity that produces them.

John Rigler · Sources checked 21 October 2026

The concern about small-town speed traps has two connected parts. Motorists are asked to pay, often while facing record and insurance consequences. The government collecting the money may use fines and fees to support ordinary operations. Where an operating budget depends heavily on those receipts, fewer citations can become a fiscal problem.

The incentive has been officially documented

In its 2017 report, the U.S. Commission on Civil Rights identified government dependence on traffic citations as an incentive to issue more citations and fines, and warned that revenue goals can displace public safety.[9] This places the issue beyond a dispute about whether one driver deserved one ticket.

DOJ's 2015 Ferguson investigation documented a particular instance of police and municipal court practices driven by revenue generation. Its findings included unconstitutional practices, racial bias, and especially severe consequences for residents with little money.[10] Those are findings about Ferguson in the investigated period, not an attribution of the same conduct to every town.

A town's books show the scale

Henderson, Louisiana, is in the I-10 corridor described by the state's transportation department.[11] Its audited General Fund budget comparison for the year ended June 30, 2025 reports the following actual amounts:[12]

General Fund itemActual amount
Fines and forfeitures revenue$2,117,485
Total revenue$2,715,222
Public safety expenditure$1,380,849

Fines and forfeitures supplied about 78.0% of General Fund revenue: $2,117,485 divided by $2,715,222. The denominator is this fund's revenue; other town funds and financing sources are separate. The fine category aggregates fines and forfeitures. Citation-level records would be needed to attribute receipts to speeding or I-10 stops. Public safety is the expenditure category shown in the schedule.

The audit establishes substantial financial reliance on the category. It does not establish whether a particular stop was justified, whether a quota existed, or how a specific insurance outcome was negotiated. Those questions require their own records.

Follow the money on the interstate

A driver's total payment can be divided among fines, court costs, state funds, local funds, and other recipients. Louisiana R.S. 32:266, for example, directs fines for interstate speeding of less than ten miles per hour over the limit, collected by the covered local bodies in jurisdictions without a home rule charter, to the Louisiana Highway Safety Fund.[13] The jurisdiction and statutory allocation determine which institution retains the revenue.

That is why an interstate ticket's price and the local police budget should not simply be treated as the same number. The useful inquiry follows the actual remittances and retained amounts, then compares them with the budget, enforcement locations, and safety outcomes.

What would establish a revenue-driven practice?

Useful evidence includes adopted revenue targets, internal instructions about ticket production, fine and fee receipts, transfers to other bodies, and spending supported by retained collections. Citation locations and times can be compared with crash history, traffic volume, speed-limit changes, and enforcement priorities. Comparing similarly situated defendants' offers can show how money, representation, and procedure affect the result.

The Commons' argument is that routine ticket negotiation and financial dependence can make enforcement function like a toll on motorists, including people passing through a town. Rigler's extra lawyer payment concerns access to a better outcome; this article concerns the recipient institution's incentive to collect. They are separate parts of the same inquiry.
