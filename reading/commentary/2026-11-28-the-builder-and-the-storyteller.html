The Builder and the Storyteller

Competence is not enough when society rewards the ability to tell a convincing story about competence.

LcudkPQzLuuqsnzHSmJ7iLREaHStvPKRVb · 28 November 2026 · Version 1 · Unsigned, AI-assisted commentary

Two forms of competence

One person builds a working system, solves a problem, or produces a tangible result. Another persuades people that they are the sort of person who can be entrusted with responsibility. Both activities require skill. They are not, however, the same skill, and success in one does not establish success in the other.

Here, the storyteller is not an entertainer. Storytelling means constructing and maintaining a socially credible identity: a narrative of capability, authority, belonging and future achievement. The builder is someone whose principal investment is in the work itself. These are analytical roles, not mutually exclusive classes of people.

In a profile-oriented culture, a person's publicly validated identity may travel further than their actual work. Hans-Georg Moeller and Paul J. D'Ambrosio call this mode of identity construction profilicity in You and Your Profile [1]. A professional profile is not inherently deceptive; the danger is treating the profile as sufficient proof of the abilities it describes.

Why the story can succeed

Anderson and colleagues found that overconfidence could confer social status, in part through impressions of competence [2]. A meta-analysis by Grijalva and colleagues found a positive association between narcissistic traits and leadership emergence but no overall linear association with observer-rated leadership effectiveness; the analysis also found a nonlinear relationship [3]. These findings do not imply that confident leaders are incompetent. They do show why acquiring authority and exercising it well must be evaluated separately.

Social signals are necessary when direct verification is expensive. An employer cannot thoroughly test every candidate; a collaborator must sometimes trust a promise. But when the cost of discovering a mistaken judgment falls on coworkers, customers or the next institution, the incentives for presentation and delivery can diverge.

The temporal advantage: a hypothesis

Consider someone who maintains an attractive account of themselves for months or years. During that interval they may experience trust, inclusion, resources and authority. A contradiction may eventually become impossible to ignore. The resulting embarrassment, loss or relationship rupture may be brief relative to the period in which the story worked. A new environment may even permit a new narrative. By contrast, a person who repeatedly contests unsupported claims may encounter lower-grade friction on a continuing basis.

The proposed distinction is between intermittent crises and chronic social friction. It is conceivable that a strategy with serious collective costs can nevertheless deliver subjective benefits to its practitioner. That would be social adaptation, not necessarily emotional maturity or ethical conduct.

Related research is suggestive but does not demonstrate this entire cycle. Leckelt and colleagues observed changes in popularity associated with different aspects of narcissism over time [4]. A cross-cultural meta-analysis found that grandiose narcissism was associated with higher subjective wellbeing, especially in individualistic settings, whereas vulnerable narcissism was associated with worse wellbeing [5]. Neither result establishes that deliberate deception improves lifetime health, that crises are brief, or that builders suffer more. Those are hypotheses, not clinical findings. Harm to relationships and others must also count in any assessment of wellbeing.

What the builder can learn

The answer is not to abandon evidence or become a more convincing fabricator. It is to treat communication as a second competence, with its own techniques and responsibilities.

  • Construct continuity. Explain how completed work fits into a larger purpose instead of presenting disconnected accomplishments.
  • Make value legible. Identify the audience's problem, show the working artifact and explain why the result matters.
  • Distinguish vision from accomplishment. Describe intended outcomes confidently while labeling what exists, what remains unfinished and what has not been tested.
  • Practice relevance rather than exhaustive disclosure. Be truthful, disclose material limitations and avoid burying the central point under every incidental detail.
  • Build relationships deliberately. The social infrastructure around a project is part of the work. Choose which disagreements need correction, and which can be left alone without misleading anyone or allowing harm.

For example, a person who builds a tool may present a long inventory of implementation details. A narrative-competent builder can instead explain what problem it solves, demonstrate the present capability, provide an inspectable record, and describe the next objective without claiming it is already achieved. Nothing in the second account requires inventing a fact.

Do not confuse social style with ability

There may be unequal costs to acquiring conventional social presentation skills. A 2017 study by Sasson and colleagues found less favorable first impressions of autistic participants when observers had audio or visual cues, while ratings of speech transcripts alone did not significantly differ [6]. That result concerns first impressions, not honesty or technical competence. There is no basis here for identifying builders with neurodivergence or storytellers with neurotypicality. Nor should improving communication require suppressing every natural difference in expression.

What institutions can learn

Organizations should evaluate both the account and the underlying work. Ask for demonstrations, reproducible outcomes, independent references and evidence that matches the claimed contribution. Revisit earlier promises after enough time has passed to observe delivery. Credit collaboration and maintenance, not merely visible ownership. A persuasive narrative is useful for coordinating action, but it should remain open to correction by the record.

Commons itself is an example of this proposed distinction: a story can explain why a record matters, while an inspectable record allows others to challenge that story. A content hash identifies bytes, not their truth. Neither polished prose nor cryptographic provenance substitutes for substantive verification.

A research question

A useful longitudinal study would track independently assessed work, confidence and narrative skill, selection into authority, delivery against commitments, social support, stress and relationship changes. It would test whether discrepancies between presentation and performance predict delayed crises, whether high mobility changes the consequences, and whether accurate storytelling helps competent participants gain recognition without increasing misrepresentation. It would also account for opportunity, resources, discrimination, work role, and the possibility that strong communicators are effective builders as well.

The builder does not need to become the opposite of the builder. The builder needs to become competent at telling the story of the work, while keeping the story answerable to the work.
