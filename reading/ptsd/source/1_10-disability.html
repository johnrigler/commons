PTSD, functional limitations, and disability benefits

PTSD can be a basis for disability benefits when its documented effects meet the program's requirements. Assessment, work capacity, and access to care are separate parts of that question.

John Rigler · 22 October 2026 · AI-assisted research and editing

Post-traumatic stress disorder can follow experiences such as assault, abuse, a serious accident, a disaster, or military trauma. It can affect civilians and veterans. Symptoms can involve intrusive memories, avoidance, disturbed sleep, heightened alertness, and changes in mood or thinking.[1] Their effects on everyday activities and sustained work provide important evidence in a disability claim.

A diagnosis and a benefits decision answer different questions

NIMH describes PTSD diagnosis as involving symptoms lasting longer than a month and interfering with daily life, with assessment by a mental-health professional.[1] Adult Social Security disability claims apply a separate standard: inability to perform substantial gainful activity because of a medically determinable impairment lasting, or expected to last, at least twelve continuous months, or expected to result in death.[2] The expected-duration rule means someone need not wait for twelve months to pass before applying.

SSDI ordinarily also requires sufficient covered work history. SSI has income, resource, and other eligibility conditions.[3][2] Military service is not a prerequisite for Social Security disability benefits.[8] A PTSD diagnosis can support a claim; the decision still needs evidence addressing the applicable functional and nonmedical requirements.

Social Security's trauma-related listing has two routes

Adult listing 12.15 covers trauma- and stressor-related disorders. Paragraph A requires medical documentation of specified exposure, re-experiencing, avoidance, mood/behavior changes, and increased arousal or reactivity. The listing then requires either paragraph B or paragraph C.[4]

Listing routeAdditional showing
A and BAn extreme limitation in one, or marked limitations in two, of four areas: using information, social interaction, sustaining attention and pace, and adaptation or self-management.
A and CA medically documented history of at least two years, ongoing treatment or support that reduces symptoms, and minimal capacity to adapt to new demands or environmental changes.

The full rule defines these terms and conditions.[4] The two-year history belongs to the paragraph C route; it is not a universal waiting period for PTSD claims. If a severe impairment does not meet or medically equal a listing, SSA continues to evaluate remaining work capacity and the ability to perform past or other work.[3]

Connect symptoms to the demands of daily work

A usable record describes what happens, how often, how long it lasts, and what help is required. For example, interrupted sleep may affect reliable attendance or concentration; reminders of trauma may disrupt particular tasks; avoidance may affect travel or contact with others. These are illustrations of questions to investigate, not assumptions about everyone with PTSD.

Reports can identify changes over time, missed or shortened work periods, difficulty completing tasks, treatment effects, and the support that makes an activity possible. A successful appointment or an activity performed with extensive help does not describe an entire workweek. SSA's guidance calls for assessing sustained functioning in context, including structured settings and support.[4]

Medical evidence must establish the impairment. Once it is established, information from the applicant and others can help explain severity and functioning.[5] The disability-record article explains how to organize provider information, reports, work demands, and outstanding evidence requests.

A gap in care needs an explanation

SSA's symptom-evaluation ruling requires considering possible reasons for limited treatment before using that history to find reported symptoms inconsistent with the record. Examples include inability to afford care and mental limitations affecting the ability to understand or pursue treatment.[6] This requires examination of the circumstances; it does not automatically resolve the claim or remove the need for medical evidence.

SSA also tells applicants not to delay filing while waiting for medical records. It requests evidence from identified sources and may arrange an examination at its expense when information is insufficient.[7] Recording why evidence is missing, what was requested, and who is obtaining it helps distinguish an unfinished record from a substantive finding about disability.

VA compensation is a separate system

For qualifying veterans, VA PTSD compensation examines the diagnosis and its connection to service. VA uses a disability-rating system; SSA applies its own eligibility rules and work-related standard. A VA award does not itself establish entitlement to SSDI, and the applications are separate.[9][10][8] The label “disability” should not obscure which agency is deciding which benefit.

When the process adds another burden

An application can require repeated calls, unfamiliar appointments, detailed accounts of distressing events, and responses to deadlines. Where symptoms make those tasks harder, the procedure can interfere with obtaining the evidence the agency needs. This is a reason to examine assistance, clear requests, and avoidable repetition. It does not establish that every difficult procedure was designed to exclude applicants.

The connected articles on application assistance, review, and New Mexico's programs describe further routes and responsibilities. Private clinical and trauma records belong in the appropriate case file. The public Commons entry supplies an explanation without requiring publication of a person's experiences or medical evidence.

This entry focuses on adult benefit claims. Childhood SSI uses a different disability standard. Treatment decisions and benefit eligibility are separate matters; no individual diagnosis or eligibility finding is made here.
