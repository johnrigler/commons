Municipal finance · Enforcement incentives

When citations fund the town

A government that depends on enforcement receipts has a financial interest in the activity that produces them.

John Rigler · 22 October 2026 · AI-assisted research and editing

Revenue dependence changes the incentive

The U.S. Commission on Civil Rights' 2017 report found that dependence on traffic citations creates incentives to issue more citations and warned that raising money can displace public safety. It treated court and government reliance on fines and fees as a conflict-of-interest problem.[23] The institutional question extends beyond whether a particular driver committed an offense: what happens to the budget if fewer people are cited?

Internal records can connect the budget to enforcement

DOJ's 2015 Ferguson investigation documented revenue expectations communicated through city leadership to police and court operations. It found that the revenue emphasis shaped enforcement and contributed to unlawful practices, with especially serious consequences for Black residents.[24] Those findings concern Ferguson in the investigated period. They also show the kind of evidence that can establish a revenue-driven practice: budgets, correspondence, assignments, and court operations considered together.

A town's accounts show financial reliance

Henderson, Louisiana's audited General Fund budget comparison for the year ended 30 June 2025 reports these actual amounts:[25]

General Fund itemActual amount
Fines and forfeitures revenue$2,117,485
Total revenue$2,715,222
Public safety expenditure$1,380,849

Fines and forfeitures supplied about 78.0% of that fund's revenue, calculated as $2,117,485 divided by $2,715,222. The category includes fines and forfeitures generally; it does not identify every dollar as a speeding or interstate-ticket receipt. Other funds and financing sources are outside this denominator.

Follow retained revenue and compare safety results

A ticket payment can be divided among fines, court costs, state funds, local funds, and other recipients. The relevant budget measure is what the institution retains and spends. Citation locations and production targets can then be compared with crashes, traffic volume, and stated enforcement priorities.

Financial dependence establishes an incentive. A finding that enforcement was improperly directed requires the operational evidence as well. Keeping those questions distinct makes the criticism stronger: it identifies the funding structure and then tests how the structure affected decisions.

A safety system should be evaluated by safety outcomes. Funding ordinary operations through the volume of penalties creates a competing measure of success, especially when the people paying have little political influence over the collecting jurisdiction.
